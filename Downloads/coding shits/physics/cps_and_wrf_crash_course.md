# Crash Course: CPS & WRF Analysis in Nuclear Fusion

If your guide works with **CPS** and **WRF**, she is analyzing the charged particles (protons, deuterons, and alpha particles) that fly out of a nuclear fusion explosion. By measuring these particles, she can determine if the fusion experiment was successful, how hot it got, and how tightly the fuel was compressed.

Both of these tools rely heavily on a special type of plastic called **CR-39**. When a high-energy proton hits this plastic, it leaves a microscopic crater (a "track"). By etching the plastic in acid and scanning it under a microscope, researchers can count the craters and measure their sizes to understand the physics of the explosion.

Here is a breakdown of what each tool does and how her code works.

---

## 1. CPS (Charged-Particle Spectrometer)

### The Physics
A CPS uses a giant magnet to bend the path of the charged particles before they hit the CR-39 plastic detector. Because lighter or faster particles are harder to bend, they hit different locations on the plastic. This creates a "spectrum" (a graph showing how many particles there are at each energy level).

### The Software (`cps-analysis`)
The `cps-analysis` code takes the raw scanned data from the CR-39 and turns it into readable physics graphs.

**Workflow:**
1. **Extract the Spectrum (`analyze_cps.py`)**: You run this script and give it the data files. It pops up a graph showing the craters. You manually click to draw boxes around the "background noise" (empty space) and the "signal" (the actual particles). It outputs a `.csv` spreadsheet of the spectrum.
2. **Calculate Yield & Energy (`analyze_spectra.py`)**: You run this script on the `.csv` you just made. You click the edges of the peak on a graph, and it calculates the total **Yield** (how many particles were produced) and the **Mean Energy** (how fast they were going).

---

## 2. WRF (Wedge Range Filter) Spectrometer

### The Physics
Unlike a CPS which uses magnets, a WRF uses a wedge-shaped piece of metal placed in front of the CR-39 plastic. Particles hitting the thick part of the wedge lose more energy than particles hitting the thin part. By measuring which particles made it through to the plastic, researchers can calculate the proton spectrum.

WRFs are specifically used to calculate **ρR (Areal Density)**. This is a measurement of how tightly the nuclear fuel was compressed before it exploded. High ρR = good fusion.

### The Software (`WRF_Analysis`)
This repository is much more automated and ties directly into the massive database at the **National Ignition Facility (NIF)** (the world's largest laser, located in California).

**Workflow:**
1. **Download NIF Data (`load_info_from_nif_database.py`)**: Instead of doing things manually, this script automatically logs into the NIF database using a shot ID (e.g., `N210808-001`). It downloads all the temperatures, yields, and coordinates of the diagnostics in the laser chamber.
2. **Etching & Scanning**: She sends physical work orders (generated by the script) to a lab technician to etch the CR-39 plastic in acid and scan it.
3. **Calculating ρR and Plotting (`make_plots_from_analysis.py`)**: Once the CR-39 is scanned and analyzed, this script takes the proton data, corrects for the thickness of the target capsule (hohlraum), and calculates the final ρR. It automatically generates presentation-ready plots and spreadsheets to send to the lead scientists.

---

### In Summary:
- **CPS** is for manual, hands-on analysis of particle tracks bent by magnets to find yields and energies.
- **WRF** is a heavily automated pipeline for downloading data from the world's biggest laser, analyzing it, and calculating how tightly the fusion fuel was compressed (ρR).
